01 THE INSPIRATION

The person who
changed how we
saw a wheelchair.

Our visit to SVNIRTAR and meeting Khusi changed the way we thought about mobility, safety and independence.

01

BEFORE THE VISIT

Before we built, we had to listen.

When we first began working on SAKHAA, our conversations sounded like typical engineering conversations.

We discussed motors, batteries, sensors, controllers, communication systems and artificial intelligence. We studied existing products and research papers and thought about the different ways technology could make a wheelchair more capable.

We wanted to build something that could support independence while also providing intelligent assistance.

But there was one thing we had not done enough of: spending time with the people who actually live with the realities we were trying to solve.

“We went to understand wheelchairs. We came back understanding the person sitting in one.”
02

THE VISIT

A visit to SVNIRTAR.

Our visit to the Swami Vivekanand National Institute of Rehabilitation Training and Research became an important moment in the project.

We did not go there simply to demonstrate an idea. We went to listen, observe and understand what a wheelchair means in everyday life.

The visit became a form of field research for us. Not because one conversation could answer every question, but because it could challenge assumptions that a laboratory or a datasheet could not.

03

THE PERSON BEHIND THE MACHINE

Meeting Khusi.

Among the people we met was Khusi, a 17-year-old girl who had been dependent on a wheelchair since childhood.

When we first saw her, it was easy to notice the wheelchair. But as the conversation developed, the wheelchair itself became less important than the person using it.

Communication with Khusi was not always easy because she had difficulty expressing herself clearly through speech. Much of our conversation therefore happened through her mother.

At first, this seemed like a practical communication issue. Gradually, it became a lesson in accessibility itself.

WHAT WE REALISED

Accessibility is not only about moving from one place to another. It is also about being able to communicate, express a need, make a choice and have someone willing to listen.

THE QUESTION CHANGED
BEFORE

What features can we add?

AFTER

What does a person actually need?

04 THE TURNING POINT

The moment everything changed.

Then the conversation took a turn we did not expect.

During our interaction, we learned that Khusi had experienced an accident while using her wheelchair. As she spoke about what had happened, she became emotional and started crying.

For a few moments, the discussion about technology felt secondary.

We had been talking about motors, sensors, communication and intelligent control. But seeing the emotional effect of that experience made us think about the problem differently.

THE QUESTION WE STARTED ASKING

What if a wheelchair could do more to protect the person using it?

05

FROM MOBILITY TO SAFETY

Safety became part of the design.

The accident did not give us a ready-made engineering solution. Instead, it gave safety a much stronger place in our design thinking.

We began considering how SAKHAA could detect obstacles, manage speed, provide emergency alerts and create additional layers of protection when something unexpected happens.

Ideas such as obstacle detection, controlled speed, emergency response and a protective airbag mechanism became more meaningful to us.

They were no longer simply interesting features to add to a prototype. They were responses to a real concern that we had heard and witnessed.

SAFETY BY DESIGN

Safety should not be an optional feature added at the end.

The proposed airbag is intended as an additional protective mechanism, not a guarantee against injury.

06

FROM EXPERIENCE TO ENGINEERING

How Khusi's story influenced SAKHAA.

Her experience did not tell us exactly what hardware to buy or what algorithm to write. It changed the questions we wanted the system to answer.

01

Mobility control

Support controlled movement and user-directed navigation.

02

Obstacle awareness

Detect nearby hazards and reduce avoidable collision risk.

03

Speed management

Allow movement to be controlled according to the situation.

04

Emergency response

Provide alerts and response mechanisms during critical events.

05

Protective mechanism

Add an additional layer of protection through the proposed airbag concept.

06

Connected care

Keep caregivers informed when assistance is required.

07 / INDEPENDENCE WITHOUT ISOLATION

More autonomy. Still connected.

The experience also made us think about the importance of caregiver connection.

Independence should not mean isolation. A person should have more control over movement while still having access to support when it is genuinely needed.

Instead of requiring constant physical monitoring, a connected wheelchair can be designed to provide information when attention is actually needed.

The goal is not to replace the caregiver. The goal is to give the user greater autonomy while preserving a safety net.

08

WHAT KHUSI TAUGHT US

The human behind the design.

One conversation changed the direction of our project.

Before visiting SVNIRTAR, we thought our challenge was mainly to make a wheelchair smarter. After meeting Khusi, we understood that intelligence alone is not enough.

A wheelchair can contain sensors, motors, controllers, communication systems and artificial intelligence. But if the technology is disconnected from the lived experience of the person using it, technical sophistication does not automatically become meaningful assistance.

Khusi's experience reminded us that behind every engineering requirement is a real person.

At the same time, we do not see Khusi's story as proof that one design can solve the challenges faced by every wheelchair user. Different people have different abilities, environments, preferences and support systems.

SAKHAA

Person first.
Wheelchair second.

Technology in service of both.
