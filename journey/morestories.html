READ MORE STORIES 01 / A CONVERSATION
DIKSHA DINDE

What we learned
from listening.

A conversation about disability, mobility, accessibility, independence and what assistive technology needs to get right.

01

Our conversation with Diksha Dinde made us look at mobility differently.

Diksha is a woman with a disability who has spent years navigating education, work, travel and everyday life while dealing with environments that are often not designed with people with disabilities in mind.

Her experiences gave us something that research papers and product specifications cannot provide on their own: a perspective on what it actually feels like to depend on mobility devices in everyday life.

The conversation was not about one particular wheelchair or one particular product. It was about understanding the person, the environment around them and the gap between what technology can offer and what people actually need.

02
BEFORE MOBILITY

Sometimes the biggest barrier isn't the device.

For years, Diksha avoided using a wheelchair because of the reactions that came with it.

She spoke about the pity, intrusive questions, unsolicited advice and assumptions that people often make when they see someone using a mobility device.

There were also situations where inaccessible places meant that her family or friends had to physically carry her. Experiences like these can make a person feel as though they are the problem, when in reality the environment is what has failed to accommodate them.

WHAT WE TOOK FROM THIS

Accessibility is not only about making a device accessible. It is also about the spaces, systems and attitudes surrounding the person using it.

03
FREEDOM

Mobility devices can change what is possible.

When Diksha eventually started using a manual wheelchair and later an electric mobility device, the experience changed.

The device was not simply a way of moving from one place to another. It gave her more freedom to decide where she wanted to go and how she wanted to get there.

That distinction was important for us.

Assistive technology should not automatically be thought of as a symbol of dependence. When designed properly, it can reduce dependence and give a person more control over their own everyday life.

04
THE PRACTICAL SIDE

Advanced technology can still create barriers.

One of the strongest points from the conversation was that adding more technology does not automatically make an assistive device better.

A powered wheelchair can offer greater independence, but it can also become difficult to use if it is too heavy, too expensive, difficult to transport or poorly suited to the environment where it is being used.

A device might work extremely well in a controlled demonstration and still become frustrating when someone has to take it onto a train, move it between locations or use it in spaces that were never designed around wheelchair access.

That made practicality just as important as technology.

05
WHAT MAKES A DEVICE USEFUL?

Not just smarter. More usable.

01
Lightweight

Easier to move, handle and transport.

02
Portable

A mobility device should fit into real travel and everyday situations.

03
Customizable

Seating, leg support, controls and positioning need to adapt to the person.

04
Practical

Technology should work within the environments where people actually live.

06
DESIGNED AROUND THE PERSON

One wheelchair cannot fit everyone.

Diksha also spoke about how much customization can matter.

Seat width and depth, leg and foot-rest height, body positioning and hand-rest placement can all affect comfort and posture.

These details may seem small from the outside, but they can have a major impact on how someone uses a wheelchair throughout the day.

She also described a powered wheelchair that weighed around 17.5 kilograms including its battery and could fold in a suitcase-like manner. For someone who has to travel frequently, that difference in portability can completely change how practical the device becomes.

07
UNIVERSAL DESIGN

Accessibility has to be built in.

One example was the placement of controls.

If a joystick is fixed permanently to one side, someone who cannot use that hand may immediately face a limitation.

A control that can be moved to the other side can make the same system usable for a wider range of people.

The same principle applies to other parts of a mobility device.

Instead of designing for one assumed user and adding accessibility later, accessibility should be considered from the beginning.

08
SAFETY & INDEPENDENCE

Independence should not mean unnecessary risk.

Our conversation also touched on the balance between independence and safety.

Features such as automatic braking, speed limits and anti-rollback mechanisms can help in situations where a user needs additional control.

At the same time, electronic systems can fail. That makes manual and mechanical safety mechanisms important as well.

The goal is not to replace a person's ability or decision-making with technology. It is to give them useful support when they need it.

09
BEYOND THE DEVICE

Accessibility is also about dignity.

Diksha challenged another assumption we often see around disability: the idea that disabled people should automatically be treated as inspirational simply for doing ordinary things.

Appreciation, she explained, should be connected to a specific achievement rather than to the fact that someone with a disability is simply living their life.

That distinction matters because people with disabilities do not need pity or constant admiration. They need the same things everyone needs — dignity, equality, access, choice and the ability to participate in everyday life.

10
THE BIGGER PICTURE

This is not a niche problem.

Diksha's experiences also made us think beyond wheelchair users alone.

Disability can enter someone's life through accidents, illness, ageing or changes in their environment. As populations age, the need for mobility and care solutions is likely to become relevant to more families.

She also spoke about the importance of data and evidence. Without understanding how people actually live, travel and interact with their surroundings, it becomes difficult to design solutions that address real problems.

Technology can contribute to that solution, but it cannot solve physical, social and institutional barriers on its own.

WHAT WE CARRY FORWARD

This conversation gave us a different way to think about assistive technology.

It is not simply about putting more intelligence, sensors or electronics into a wheelchair.

It is about understanding the person, the environment and the life around the device.

Mobility should be practical. Safety should be considered from the beginning. Devices should be customizable and affordable. Accessibility should not be an afterthought.

Most importantly, technology should support independence without taking away choice.

A CONVERSATION WE WILL CARRY INTO THE DESIGN.
